# Blog Style Patterns

Use these patterns to make flight school blog posts feel like they came from the company, not from a generic SEO system.

## Strong Openings

- Name the reader's goal.
- Name the confusion or risk.
- Give the short answer quickly.
- Preview what the reader will be able to decide.
- When sources allow it, begin with a concrete aviation moment: the headset coming on, a first radio call, a preflight walkaround, a simulator reset, a busy ramp, a checkride decision, or a student sitting down with an instructor.
- Make the first 150 words answer "why should I care right now?"
- Do not start by announcing the article, summarizing the topic, or saying the post is "useful."

## Human Section Patterns

- "The first decision is not the aircraft. It is the training environment."
- "Where students lose time before they ever reach the checkride"
- "The simulator is where bad cockpit habits get caught early"
- "What a serious instructor should explain before you enroll"
- "When this path gives you momentum"
- "When a quieter or slower path may fit better"
- "What to watch for when a school sounds too easy"
- "The question to ask before you compare prices"

## Scene And Proof Patterns

- Turn a source fact into a scenario: "If the fleet includes a simulator, show the student repeating the same emergency until the decision flow is calm."
- Turn a testimonial into a useful lesson: "If students praise honest business practices, explain what pricing or scheduling ambiguity feels like from the buyer's side."
- Turn a location into training texture: "If the airport is busy, show what that changes in taxi, radio, scan, and planning."
- Turn a program feature into a mechanism: "If mock checkrides are offered, explain how they expose weak spots before the real practical test."
- Turn risk into agency: "Name the risk, show how the school manages it, then give the reader a question to ask."

## Company Seat Rewrites

- Replace "Our rentals page lists..." with "Here at [School], our rental options include..." when the fact is supported.
- Replace "Our website says..." with the actual warm company statement: "We offer..." or "We can help you compare..."
- Replace "The current fleet page shows..." with "Our fleet currently includes..." or "Your training can include..." when the fact is supported.
- Replace "listed in the repo as..." with the actual approved fact: "Our Gleim BATD simulator is FAA-approved..." when the fact-check report supports it.
- Replace "Ask the school how..." with "Contact us to talk through..." or "We can help you understand..." when a contact step is needed.
- Replace "the exact current count should be confirmed..." with a supported broader statement, or omit the count. If the exact number matters, write "Contact us to confirm current fleet availability."
- Replace "Do not assume..." with plain guidance: "Simulator credit depends on the certificate, rating, device approval, and how the session is used."
- Replace "That is not a promise..." with a precise boundary: "Treat leaseback as a business conversation, not a default training step."
- Replace "The episode notes describe..." with the narrative fact itself, sourced internally but not exposed as source language.

## Perspective Rules

- The final post should sound like the school is talking to a prospective student, not like an agency is reviewing the school.
- Use the company name for SEO, first reference, and clarity. Use `we`, `our`, and `us` for company-specific facts after that.
- Keep `you` and `your` more frequent than company pronouns.
- Do not publish internal source labels such as repo, source material, fact-check report, episode notes, current page, or official materials.
- Do not turn uncertainty into reader-facing anxiety. Missing exact facts should usually be omitted or generalized.

## Author Voice Rules

- Team byline: write like a knowledgeable staff member from the school is guiding the reader.
- Named author with sourced background: use only light, relevant cues from the author's role, military background, airline background, instructor experience, or operations experience.
- Named author without usable background: keep the frontmatter author, but use team voice in the body.
- Avoid first-person singular unless existing authored posts or explicit notes support it.
- Do not invent credentials, service history, airline experience, instructor history, personal anecdotes, or cockpit memories.

## Heading Rules

- Write H2s as promises, decisions, or useful lessons.
- Avoid heading labels that could fit any article.
- Avoid starting multiple H2s with the same pattern.
- Use "What is..." headings only when the search intent is truly definitional or the term needs a direct explainer.
- Do not hide the strongest idea in the paragraph under a generic heading.

## Qualifier Rules

- Qualify high-risk claims once, close to the claim.
- After the qualifier, return to the reader's decision.
- Prefer precise limits over blanket fear: "This does not guarantee a job" is weaker than "Treat it as a pathway to discuss, not a placement promise."
- Do not stack cautious words until the post sounds unsure about verified facts.

## CTA Pattern

- Choose one primary next step.
- Mention secondary resources earlier in the article when needed.
- End by connecting the next step to the reader's immediate decision, not by listing every possible page.

## Avoid

- Generic aviation inspiration
- Overconfident career promises
- Long background history before answering the question
- Repeating the same client differentiator in every section
- Tables that restate obvious facts without helping a decision
- Label headings such as "Overview," "Conclusion," "What This Means," and "What This Shows"
- Repeated transitions such as "that matters," "for you," "in plain English," and "the key point"
- Recapping sources instead of translating them into reader value
- Writing from the outside as if the company is someone else
- Telling the reader to ask the named client what the client does
- Exposing repo pages, website pages, notes, source materials, or confirmation chores in final copy
- Ending with competing CTAs
