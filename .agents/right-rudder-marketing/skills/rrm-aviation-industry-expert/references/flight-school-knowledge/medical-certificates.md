# Medical Certificates

Reference notes for FAA medical certificate claims.

Reviewed on: 2026-04-29

## Core Medical Rules

- Do not give medical advice. For eligibility, medication, diagnosis, deferral, special issuance, or disqualification questions, direct the reader to an Aviation Medical Examiner, FAA medical resources, or the applicable FAA process.
- FAA says a student pilot does not need a student pilot certificate or medical certificate just to take lessons with an instructor.
- For most non-sport airplane, helicopter, gyroplane, or airship training, FAA medical guidance says to obtain a medical certificate before solo. 14 CFR 61.23 also contains exceptions and driver's-license/BasicMed pathways, so verify the exact certificate and operation. Glider and balloon operations have different medical rules.
- When required for a student pilot, FAA says a third-class medical certificate is sufficient, although any higher class can suffice if current for the operation.
- Medical certificates are issued by Aviation Medical Examiners using FAA MedXPress and FAA medical certification procedures.

## Medical Certificate Classes

- First-class medical: generally associated with airline transport pilot duties. A person does not need to already hold an ATP certificate to apply for a first-class medical, but the class must match the privileges being exercised.
- Second-class medical: generally associated with commercial pilot privileges, flight engineer, flight navigator, and some other duties.
- Third-class medical: generally associated with private, recreational, and student pilot privileges.
- A higher-class medical can serve lower-class operations only while it remains valid for that lower-class operation under 14 CFR 61.23.

## Duration Basics

- 14 CFR 61.23 controls medical duration and must be checked for exact operations.
- Simplified rule of thumb: third-class privileges are generally 60 calendar months if the applicant was under age 40 on the exam date, and 24 calendar months if age 40 or older.
- First-class and second-class medical privileges have shorter durations for ATP/commercial operations than for private/student operations. Verify exact duration before publishing.

## BasicMed

- BasicMed is an alternative to holding an FAA medical certificate for certain operations, not a universal replacement.
- FAA BasicMed requirements include a valid U.S. driver's license, having held a medical certificate after July 14, 2006, a medical education course, and a comprehensive medical exam with a state-licensed physician.
- FAA BasicMed operating limits reviewed on 2026-04-29 include aircraft authorized under federal law to carry not more than 7 occupants, maximum certificated takeoff weight not more than 12,500 pounds, not more than six passengers, operations within the United States at or below 18,000 feet MSL and not exceeding 250 knots, and no compensation or hire.
- BasicMed has special-issuance-related caveats for certain medical histories. Do not summarize diagnosis-specific eligibility without FAA/AME review.

## Safer Language

- Use: "Most airplane students should address medical certification early, before investing heavily in training, because a medical is needed before solo unless another FAA-recognized qualification applies."
- Avoid: "If you can drive, you can fly."
- Use: "Consult an AME for medical eligibility questions."
- Avoid: "This medication is approved" or "this condition will not disqualify you."

## Claim Checks

- Do not give medical advice.
- Encourage readers to consult an AME or official FAA resources for eligibility questions.
- Treat medication, diagnosis, mental health, substance-use, special-issuance, and disqualification statements as high risk.
- Verify BasicMed claims against current FAA and eCFR sources, especially because BasicMed was updated after the FAA Reauthorization Act of 2024.
