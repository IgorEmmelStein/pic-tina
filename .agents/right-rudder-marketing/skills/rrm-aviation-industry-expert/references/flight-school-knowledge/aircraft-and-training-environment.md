# Aircraft And Training Environment

Reference notes for aircraft, airport, avionics, and training environment claims.

Reviewed on: 2026-04-29

## Training Aircraft Terminology

- A primary trainer is an aircraft commonly used for initial flight training. Do not imply a specific model is universally best unless the claim is framed as school-specific or mission-specific.
- Common trainer attributes include predictable handling, durable landing gear, lower operating cost, good parts/support availability, and suitability for the certificate or rating being taught.
- Complex airplane, high-performance airplane, tailwheel airplane, and other aircraft-characteristic claims can trigger specific training and endorsement rules under 14 CFR 61.31. Verify before using in copy.
- Technically advanced aircraft, glass cockpit, GPS, autopilot, and integrated avionics claims are equipment-specific. Verify against the client's current fleet and avionics configuration.
- Multi-engine training introduces asymmetric thrust, engine-out procedures, Vmc awareness, and multiengine performance/limitations. The FAA Airplane Flying Handbook has a dedicated multiengine transition chapter.

## Avionics And Glass Cockpit Language

- "Glass cockpit" generally means electronic flight displays rather than traditional round gauges, but exact equipment varies widely by aircraft.
- Avoid saying glass cockpit training is automatically safer or better. Safer framing: "helps students become familiar with modern avionics used in many aircraft."
- Simulator, aviation training device, and flight training device claims need exact device type and approval status if credit toward FAA requirements is implied.

## Towered And Non-Towered Airports

- A towered airport has an operating control tower and requires communication/compliance with ATC procedures applicable to the airspace.
- A non-towered airport does not mean "uncontrolled" in every sense; aircraft may use CTAF, published traffic patterns, and right-of-way procedures, and the surrounding airspace may still be controlled or uncontrolled depending on location.
- Private pilot airplane training includes exposure to controlled airport operations. For airplane single-engine Part 61 applicants, 14 CFR 61.109 includes takeoff and landing requirements at an airport with an operating control tower.
- Avoid saying towered or non-towered training is universally better. Towered airports can build radio and ATC confidence; non-towered airports can build self-announcing, traffic scanning, and pattern-discipline habits.

## Weather, VFR, IFR, VMC, And IMC

- VFR means visual flight rules; IFR means instrument flight rules. VMC and IMC describe meteorological conditions, not the flight plan itself.
- Under 14 CFR 61.3(e), a pilot generally needs the appropriate instrument rating to act as PIC under IFR or in weather below VFR minimums.
- Weather and dispatch claims are local, seasonal, and operationally specific. Do not promise year-round flying or guaranteed training continuity without client data.

## Client-Specific Claims

- Fleet size, aircraft models, avionics, maintenance practices, dispatch reliability, hangar space, airport environment, fuel availability, DPE access, and training routes must come from current client sources.
- If a claim says students train in "new aircraft," "the safest fleet," "airline-style avionics," or "the best airport environment," mark it risky unless the client provides proof and the wording is qualified.

## Claim Checks

- Fleet claims must be client-specific and current.
- Avoid saying one aircraft type, avionics package, airport, or training environment is universally best.
- Explain technical terms in plain language for prospective students.
- Verify simulator/ATD credit claims before use.
- Do not treat aircraft availability, weather, or dispatch reliability as guaranteed.
