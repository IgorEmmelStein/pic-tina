# Safety And Maintenance

Reference notes for safety, maintenance, and operational claims.

Reviewed on: 2026-04-29

## Safety Claim Rules

- Treat safety claims as high risk. Aviation safety language should be specific, operational, and evidence-backed.
- Avoid absolute claims such as "safest," "accident-free," "guaranteed safe," "risk-free," or "zero-risk."
- Prefer concrete practices: structured checklists, recurrent instructor standardization, documented maintenance tracking, safety briefings, weather minimums, dispatch procedures, risk management, and FAASTeam/WINGS participation when verified.
- If a claim implies legal compliance, verify against FAA/eCFR and current client documentation.

## Maintenance Terminology

- Airworthy means the aircraft conforms to its type design and is in a condition for safe operation. Avoid using "airworthy" as a casual synonym for "looks good."
- 14 CFR 91.7 says no person may operate a civil aircraft unless it is in an airworthy condition, and the PIC is responsible for determining whether the aircraft is in condition for safe flight.
- 14 CFR 91.403 places primary responsibility for maintaining an aircraft in an airworthy condition on the owner or operator.
- 14 CFR 91.409 contains inspection requirements, including annual inspections and 100-hour inspection rules that commonly matter for flight instruction aircraft provided for hire.
- 14 CFR 91.417 covers maintenance record requirements. Part 43 covers maintenance, preventive maintenance, rebuilding, alteration, and approval for return to service.

## In-House Vs Outsourced Maintenance

- In-house maintenance is not automatically safer than outsourced maintenance. Outsourced maintenance is not automatically worse.
- Safe claims need specifics: certificated mechanics, inspection tracking, discrepancy reporting, parts control, quality assurance, maintenance recordkeeping, and compliance with required inspections and airworthiness directives.
- Client-specific claims about maintenance staff, maintenance facilities, dispatch reliability, aircraft downtime, or inspection intervals must come from current client sources.

## Dispatch Reliability And Operational Claims

- Dispatch reliability depends on aircraft availability, maintenance events, weather, scheduling, instructor availability, and operational risk decisions.
- Avoid guaranteeing students will never lose a lesson to maintenance or weather.
- If a client wants to mention reliability, use current internal metrics and define the metric clearly.

## Safer Language

- Use: "The school uses documented maintenance tracking and preflight/dispatch procedures to support safe operations."
- Avoid: "Our fleet is the safest in the region."
- Use: "Students learn risk management, checklist discipline, weather decision-making, and preflight inspection habits from the beginning."
- Avoid: "Flying is completely safe."

## Claim Checks

- Treat safety claims as high risk.
- Avoid safest, accident-free, guaranteed safe, risk-free, or similar absolute claims unless legal/client review approves and the claim is fully supported.
- Prefer operational specifics over broad safety promises.
- Verify in-house maintenance, mechanic credentials, maintenance intervals, inspection compliance, aircraft availability, and safety-program claims with client documents.
- Do not use accident-rate comparisons without current, reputable, apples-to-apples data and legal review.
