# Voice And Message Map

Extract voice from existing content and translate it into practical writing guidance that future blog posts can actually use.

## Voice Dimensions

- Formal vs conversational
- Instructor-like vs peer-like
- Urgent vs steady
- Technical vs plain-English
- Institutional vs personal
- Safety-conscious vs fear-based
- Exciting vs sterile

## Message Map

Create 3 to 5 message pillars:
- Pillar name
- Core idea
- Proof
- Reader benefit
- Phrases to reuse
- Phrases to avoid

## Company Voice Sourcebook

Extract source-backed writing material, not just adjectives:
- Author voice and byline strategy: whether posts should use a team byline or a named author, what author evidence supports it, and what voice cues may be used.
- Company POV: what the school believes about training, students, safety, cost, progress, and aviation.
- Phrases that sound like the company: short phrases from current pages, interviews, testimonials, and client notes.
- Testimonial language worth echoing: student words that reveal trust, frustration, relief, momentum, or pride.
- Aviation moments available from sources: ramp, cockpit, simulator, preflight, radio, checkride, airport, fleet, instructor, or community scenes.
- Reader aspiration: what the reader wants to become, feel, solve, or prove.
- Reader fear: what could make the reader hesitate.
- Safe excitement boundaries: how to make aviation feel serious, exciting, and attainable without unsupported guarantees.
- Banned robotic phrases: repeated SEO, analyst, source-audit, and outside-reviewer phrases that do not sound like the company.
- Company-seat rewrites: examples of how the school should talk about its own fleet, airport, instructors, simulator, programs, financing, and student stories.
- Confirmation phrasing: how to invite the reader to contact the school when a current detail may change, without making the article sound unsure.

## Author Voice Rules

- If posts are credited to the school team, write like someone from the school staff is helping the reader, not like an outside agency or evaluator.
- If posts use a named author, inspect author pages, staff bios, blog frontmatter, about pages, interviews, and extra-information notes for source-backed persona cues.
- Use military, airline, instructor, maintenance, dispatch, or operations language only when source-backed and useful to the topic.
- If a named author has no usable bio or background source, keep the byline but use the team fallback voice.
- Do not invent credentials, service history, airline experience, personal anecdotes, or first-person memories.
- Do not use first-person singular unless existing authored posts or provided notes clearly support it.

## Flight School Style Notes

Good flight school copy should feel clear, grounded, confidence-building, and alive. Avoid hype when discussing safety, cost, timelines, regulation, or career outcomes, but do not drain verified facts of all energy. The reader should feel guided by an experienced aviation team, not processed through a compliance checklist.

Ban outside-reviewer phrasing in the voice sourcebook unless a direct quote requires it. Examples: "our rentals page lists," "the fleet page shows," "our website says," "the current page shows," "listed in the repo," "the episode notes describe," "ask [company] how," "the exact current count should be confirmed," "do not assume," and "not a promise."
